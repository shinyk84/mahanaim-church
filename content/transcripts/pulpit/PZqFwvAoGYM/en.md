## Financial Statements and the Break-Even Point

In the middle of all that, on Thursday, I had to go to that training. It was finance and accounting training for managers. Usually, up to staff and assistant-manager level, you just do what you're told. But from manager level on, you have to put your heads together with the department head and team leader about what the team needs, so you need to understand money — numbers.

Everyone, do you know financial statements well? Can you read them? I can't really read them either. (laughter)

What is a financial statement? Instead of describing the state of the company at great length, it presents it simply in numbers. Plus, minus — this part turned out like this, that part like that. Writing it all out at length is complicated, so it's shown in numbers. You have to know the numbers to understand what's hidden behind them. So reading financial statements becomes very important as you move up toward the executive level.

The accountant who audits our company came as the instructor. He said, "Your company is not in a very bad situation, you know." (laughter) We posted a loss, but he says it's fine. So I listened with a bit of relief.

A financial statement, he said, is a document that quantifies the state of a company in numbers and provides it to stakeholders. And stakeholders exist both inside and outside. Inside are the executives and employees, because they have to make strategy. Outside stakeholders are investors. They judge whether to keep investing. And it turns out our company is unlisted, but he said it has the scale of a listed company — it just hasn't gone public. So it also became an occasion to newly realize that our company is quite a large one.

He said financial statements consist of five parts.

- The statement of financial position: how assets, liabilities and equity are composed at a specific point in time.
- The income statement: the revenue and expenses over a certain period, usually about a year, put into numbers — it shows business performance.
- The cash flow statement: data goes back and forth, but this shows how actual cash flowed in and out.
- The statement of changes in equity: whether equity changed or not.
- The notes: the parts that are hard to express in numbers, written out in words.

So he said you have to read everything, including the notes. Since everyone there was manager level or above, he said, you should refer to these things when you need to do something within your team — but you individually also invest in stocks, right? It would be even better if you could read these. Those of you who are in the minus. (laughter)

That was financial statements, and next he talked about fixed costs and variable costs, and then about the break-even point.

Yeji, what is the break-even point? You need to know this. We need to know the basics. It's the point where costs and sales — the money coming in and the money going out — come to zero. For a company to be sustainable, it absolutely has to get past this break-even point. It can't be zero. For example, if you have ten employees each with a 50-million-won salary, the company has to earn at least 500 million won, right? But if that's all, the company goes under. If it only earns 500 million, it goes under. It has to clear more than 500 million to keep the company running. Only then can it keep investing in research with the rest, maintain the company building, and everything else. That's why you need to know the break-even point.

If you are in the red, there are three strategies: raise prices, lower fixed costs, or lower variable costs. So he said you need to know that point in order to make the right decisions. I didn't really learn these things in school, so what I had only vaguely known from studying on my own, I got to learn a bit — basic as it is.
